A case has to be made, apparently, for reading outdoors — for taking the most portable technology ever devised and using it where it works best.

The objections are always practical. The light moves. The wind interferes with the pages. There are ants. All true, and all, I want to argue, the point.

Indoors, a book gets your undivided attention, and undivided attention is overrated: it is how you read a contract, not a poem. Outdoors, the book has to share you — with the light moving, with the smell of cut grass, with the woodpigeon doing its five-note misremembering of itself. The page and the world interleave. Years later you will find you cannot remember the chapter without the weather. The book has been *situated*, the way memory researchers say, and it will stay with you the way situated things stay.

There is also the matter of pace. Rooms encourage finishing. Fields encourage stopping — to look up, to lose the line, to read the same sentence three times with an hour between readings. Some sentences only open on the third reading, in the different light. Every serious reader knows this and almost no one arranges for it. The field arranges for it.

So: a short field guide. Take a book you have already started; beginnings need desks. Sit with the sun over your left shoulder, like a lamp you cannot adjust and must instead obey. Let the bookmark be a grass stem. When something six-legged crosses the page, wait — it has right of way; it was here first.

And when the light finally goes and takes the paragraph with it, stop. Fold the corner of the evening down. The book will keep. That is the other thing books are for.
