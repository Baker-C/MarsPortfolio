There is a month — it moves, but it always comes — when the garden stops pretending. The asters go over all at once, the last bees work with a kind of administrative hurry, and everything that intends to survive begins doing so in private.

I used to think of winter as an interruption. The garden disagreed. Underground, the real schedule kept: roots banking sugar, bulbs counting cold hours the way monks count beads, seeds refusing — flatly, chemically refusing — to believe any warmth before its time. Dormancy is not sleep. It is a ledger.

The first year I kept notes, I wrote mostly absences. *No frogs. No thrum. Nothing on the wing but one late admiral, looking embarrassed.* It took me until February to understand I was describing a fullness. The field was not empty; it was holding.

We overwinter too, in our ways. There are seasons when the visible work stops and the honest response to "what are you doing lately" is *counting cold hours*. I have stopped apologizing for those seasons. The bulb does not apologize. The bulb is busy in a way that will not photograph.

What the notes taught me, in the end, was the difference between patience and waiting. Waiting watches the door. Patience sets the table anyway — lays in the sugar, seals the bud scales, does every small thing that spring will need and cannot be hurried into needing sooner.

When the snowdrops finally come up — always earlier than is sensible, always through the crust of something — they do not look surprised. They look scheduled. That is the whole lesson, and it takes a winter to read it: nothing that endures is improvising.
